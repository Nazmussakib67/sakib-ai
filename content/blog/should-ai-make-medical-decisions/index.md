## The promise of AI in healthcare

Healthcare generates enormous amounts of information: medical images, laboratory results, clinical notes, and data from wearable devices. AI can help identify patterns in these records and may assist clinicians in detecting disease, prioritizing cases, or monitoring patients.

For example, a medical-image model might flag suspicious regions in a scan for closer review. A predictive model might identify patients who need additional follow-up. These tools could be especially valuable in settings where specialists are scarce or workloads are high.

But promising results in a research dataset do not automatically mean a system is safe in a real hospital.

## The challenge of generalization

A model trained using images from one institution may encounter different scanners, patient populations, image quality, or clinical procedures elsewhere. Performance can decline when the data changes.

Accuracy also tells only part of the story. In disease detection, false negatives may delay treatment, while false positives can lead to anxiety and unnecessary testing. Researchers should evaluate sensitivity, specificity, calibration, subgroup performance, and clinical usefulness—not just a single headline score.

Patient privacy, informed consent, cybersecurity, and bias are equally important. A system that performs well overall may still work less reliably for underrepresented groups.

## Who should make the final decision?

AI can support clinical judgment, but responsibility cannot simply be delegated to a prediction. Clinicians need to understand the tool's intended use, limitations, and evidence. Hospitals need monitoring procedures. Regulators and developers need clear standards for validation and updates.

Explainable AI can sometimes help users inspect what influenced a model's prediction, but a visually appealing explanation is not proof that the model's reasoning is medically sound.

## My perspective

I am particularly interested in the potential of AI for medical imaging and cancer detection. My view is that the strongest near-term role for AI is **decision support rather than unaccountable decision replacement**.

The goal should not be to remove clinicians from care. It should be to help them detect important signals earlier, reduce avoidable workload, and make better-informed decisions. A useful healthcare AI system must be accurate, equitable, understandable enough for its setting, and rigorously tested with real clinical workflows.

**Question for readers:** Would you feel comfortable if an AI system reviewed your medical scan before a doctor did?
